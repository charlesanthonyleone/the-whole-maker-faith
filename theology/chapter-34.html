Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 34. Learning, making, play, and the offering of work

We regard learning as a spiritual obligation because a steward must understand what is being tended. Good intention cannot substitute indefinitely for competence. We begin at origins, follow relationships, practice, invite correction, and return to what we thought we understood. The Mastery Model's demand for depth protects the gathering from becoming a marketplace of impressive fragments.

We receive craft, science, cooking, repair, software, games, music, farming, and art into the religious life without making them all religious products. A song need not contain our vocabulary to bear truth. A machine need not be dedicated in a ceremony to serve a neighbor. The maker's offering is the faithful use of actual capacities in relation to a real world, not the application of a sacred label to every object.

We preserve play as an arena of discovery. Simulations and games can let us try an alternative, learn a skill, understand another perspective, or encounter an unfamiliar history. We distinguish simulated results from real-world evidence. A successful civilization model does not prove a political theology, and an engaging story does not establish the truth of its cosmology. Delight can make study possible; it cannot replace the work of verification.

The source's many proposed platforms reveal a common religious demand: make knowledge, connection, care, and opportunity more available. We receive that demand rather than canonizing every product specification. No member must use a particular app to be legible to the community. No unfinished technology is a sacrament waiting for investment.

We are especially responsible for the way we transmit insight. Expanding Light rightly asks how a serious idea can reach people who would not encounter its original form. We may translate through a story, song, game, or familiar cultural channel. We do not use concealed sponsorship, fabricated testimony, or a child's trust in an entertainer to make religious persuasion appear to be something else. Prepared teaching can be authentic; hidden manipulation is not redeemed by benevolent intentions.

Work offered to God is not unpaid labor automatically owed to the religious institution. People may decline a project, ask for compensation, protect their intellectual work, or choose a different vocation. The community's need does not prove the member's calling. We honor what can be freely offered and do not consume the person in order to display the gift.
