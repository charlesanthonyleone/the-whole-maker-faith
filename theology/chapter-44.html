Part IV. The community's limits, its witnesses, and its service to the world

Chapter 44. What a practitioner is for

We are for becoming people through whom a received world can grow more truthful, loving, and whole. We do not exist to maximize the institution's importance. The congregation is one instrument in that work, and an instrument can be replaced when it no longer serves its purpose. God is not diminished by our loss of prominence.

A practitioner is someone who keeps returning: to the source rather than the flattering image of the self; to the neighbor rather than an abstraction called humanity; to the question rather than the performance of certainty; to the entrusted thing rather than the pleasure of acquiring another. These returns are not regressions. They are how formation remains alive after its first enthusiasm passes.

We bring our brick without claiming it is the house. We allow God to be the mortar without pretending that naming the mortar has already done the building. We learn the weight, strength, history, and limits of what we carry. We let another person's gift alter the relation we thought was complete. We refuse the invitation to become whole by making that person smaller.

We give glory without disappearing from responsibility. We may say, 'I did this, and I must answer for it,' while also saying, 'I did not invent the light by which I could do it.' The source's warning about the prophet's pronouns directs us away from self-deification; it must not become a way to make God responsible for our errors. Every error in this founding work remains available for human correction. No claimed divine percentage makes it immune.

We expect the practiced life to remain particular. One member may cultivate land, another accompany the grieving, another teach children, another study a difficult problem, another make music, another rest through an illness with courage enough to receive help. Their faithfulness cannot be reduced to one output measure. The Whole Maker's ledger is not our membership dashboard.

Our common undertaking can be spoken simply: we will love the source, receive the neighbor, tell the truth we can presently see, remain open to what we cannot, tend what is entrusted, and refuse to turn another life into material for our glory. We will seek communion rather than possession. We will not declare the work complete because we have found words for it.

The founding theology ends here. The life it asks of us begins wherever something within our reach can be received, understood, protected, repaired, or formed in love.
