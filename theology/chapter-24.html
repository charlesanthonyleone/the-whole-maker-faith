Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 24. Prayer and the return of glory

We pray to the Almighty, not to an institution that claims to own access. Our ordinary prayer has three movements: acknowledgment of the source, truthful presentation of the actual condition, and willingness to undertake the next faithful act. These are a proposed aid to practice, not a revealed formula. No language, posture, hour, location, or length makes a prayer valid or invalid.

We may say: Whole Maker, you are not mine to possess. Here is what I have received; here is what I have done; here is what I do not understand. Help me receive truth without turning away from it. Show me the care within my reach, and keep me from claiming your place. We may also say nothing, remaining present without performing an answer. The person whose only honest prayer is grief has not failed to pray.

The Refinement Flame urges us to ask properly and to believe an answer may come. We receive the seriousness of that invitation. We do not turn it into a guarantee that every requested event will occur or an accusation that an unanswered prayer proves defective belief. We pray for healing without promising it, for guidance without claiming every impulse is guidance, and for the dead without pretending to administer their destination.

Silence, contemplation, singing, kneeling, standing, walking, journaling, and shared spoken prayer may all serve the return of attention to God. A practitioner may receive a contemplative practice from another tradition only with attention to its context and restrictions. We do not treat every sacred word as an interchangeable tool for producing an altered state.

The source asks the prophet to stand behind the flame rather than in front of it. We therefore return glory without making careless claims of miracle. If someone recovers, we give thanks, acknowledge the people and treatment involved, and do not certify an unexplained supernatural cause because the recovery occurred after prayer. If a project succeeds, we honor its workers rather than using divine credit to erase their labor.

The test of prayer is not whether it produces a privileged spiritual sensation. We ask whether it makes us more truthful, receptive, and capable of keeping the obligations we can actually meet. The source is not flattered by our pretending that the vessel has no cracks.
