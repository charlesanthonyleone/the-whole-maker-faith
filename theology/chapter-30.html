Founding theology

Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 30. Sacred time, celebration, and the right to rest

We recognize sacred time as attention released from the illusion that only urgent production is real. A community needs occasions to gather, remember, mourn, give thanks, and enjoy what has been made. It also needs the humility not to turn every occasion into compulsory attendance.

We establish no universal liturgical calendar, fixed reading cycle, or mandatory holy-day count. The originating writings do not supply one, and the worldwide character of this faith requires sensitivity to different seasons, inherited festivals, work patterns, and family obligations. A calendar devised now must not silently become a revelation that later communities are forbidden to adapt.

We may observe existing days of remembrance with their meanings honestly named. A community that reflects on the Cross and resurrection must explain this faith's distinctive interpretation rather than borrowing conventional Christian words to conceal it. A community joining a neighbor's festival must not imply that participation gives it ownership of the rite. We neither ban inherited celebrations nor declare them all interchangeable expressions of our own doctrine.

We encourage occasions around three kinds of reality: gratitude for what sustains life, remembrance of those whose work we inherit, and review of promises that require renewed care. A harvest meal, an anniversary of a community undertaking, a day of repair, a memorial, or a celebration of a child's growth can embody them. None needs to become an exclusive religious holiday.

We also honor pauses that have no public result. Silence is not empty inventory; sleep is not moral absence; play is not justified only by its productivity. The source's attention to sleep, acoustic wellbeing, and collective pause deepens a religious truth: a person is not a machine whose goodness increases with every additional hour extracted.

Those who cannot join an occasion must not be treated as absent from God. We plan for caregivers, shift workers, the ill, and those whose nervous systems cannot tolerate an intense gathering. A celebration becomes truthful when it makes room for real lives rather than asking those lives to disappear so the celebration can look complete.
