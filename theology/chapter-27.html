Founding theology

Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 27. The table and the four received traditions

We receive four IntelOasis traditions as practices, not sacraments. They do not transmit grace automatically, prove membership, or require a particular purchase. Each teaches an ordinary form of fidelity.

The Welcome Dish makes reception particular. We ask what the arriving person actually eats, remembers, and enjoys, then prepare something with them in mind. We do not make them consume what violates their conscience, health, or inherited practice. A recipe can carry the evening into another household. For a newborn, the dish records the family at the child's arrival; it is not food the infant must be made to eat. Hospitality begins by listening rather than using a guest as a canvas for our generosity.

The First Root makes care visible over time. A tree or suitable plant may accompany a child's growth. Adults remain responsible for its welfare until the child is genuinely able to participate. We do not let a plant die to teach a lesson or treat its condition as a judgment on the child's character. A family without land may tend a pot, join a community garden, or contribute to the care of an existing tree. The promise matters more than ownership of a yard.

The Living Tattoo makes history available to honest reflection. It may be carried on skin, in a journal, in cloth, in art, or in another freely chosen record. The source's tattoo remains an optional bodily practice for those who want it, never an initiation mark, requirement for intimacy, or reason to pressure a minor. A remembered relationship need not be erased for a new one to be real. Neither must a survivor retain a visible mark that feels like captivity. Transformation is not denial.

The First Car teaches the difference between acquiring and tending. Its spirit can be practiced with a bicycle, tool, instrument, household object, or other entrusted thing. Preserving a dangerous or irreparable machine is not required by loyalty. We learn its systems, make sound repairs, and know when responsible release is better than sentimental neglect.

At our common table we receive these practices as invitations to form habits, not a new catalogue of demands. We keep the particular source traditions recognizable while refusing to make wealth, permanent housing, bodily marking, or car ownership prerequisites for spiritual seriousness.
