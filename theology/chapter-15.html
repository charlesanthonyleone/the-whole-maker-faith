Part II. The human being, the moral world, and the life that continues

Chapter 15. The person and the irreducible calling

We believe every person exceeds the uses to which other people can put them. We call the person's distinctive configuration of capacities, sensitivities, desires, and possibilities a blueprint, receiving the term from the Organizing Function and the Blueprint writings. We do not mean a caste assignment hidden in the soul that a minister can uncover and enforce.

A calling becomes legible through attention over time: what one returns to without compulsion, where learning becomes alive, which kinds of contribution strengthen relationship, and what conditions make honest work possible. Desire is evidence, not an infallible command. Trauma, habit, fear, and addiction can imitate vocation. So can the praise offered for performing a role one does not actually inhabit. Discernment must examine the fruit as well as the intensity.

We establish companionship in vocation rather than certification of souls. A practitioner may ask two trusted people to observe a period of study or service, report what they have actually seen, and help identify a next experiment. The practitioner remains free to disagree, change direction, or stop. No prediction becomes a binding identity. No algorithmic profile becomes a divine sentence.

We make room for apparently ordinary functions. Feeding a household, repairing a machine, caring for an ill parent, translating a difficult conversation, organizing a store of tools, or maintaining a garden can carry profound vocation. Spectacular achievement is not the measure of spiritual worth. A life whose contribution cannot be made public may be deeply fruitful.

We equally refuse to require the disabled, elderly, ill, unemployed, or exhausted to exhibit useful output in order to justify care. The purpose of a person is not exhausted by production, and being loved is not a wage paid for function. Rest, dependence, and receiving can themselves expose the lie that value belongs only to those who can presently give more than they take.

The community's first task is often to change the conditions in which a person is being read. We do not call the seed empty because it has been placed on stone. We help create soil, time, safety, and opportunity, and we remain prepared for something to grow that our first account did not predict.
