Part I. God, creation, and the restoration of stewardship

Chapter 6. Rebellion as the theft of the source

We understand rebellion against God not first as inquiry, anger, or refusal of a human ruler, but as a misattribution: the instrument claims that the light passing through it originated in itself. The steward becomes the destination. Its received capacities become evidence, in its own account, that it no longer needs to receive.

The Scattering tells this through the fall of the thirteenth. The firstborn sees its brightness and tries to take the source's place. In The Beginning gives the rebellion another texture: the Lightbringer sees pain beyond the reach of his illumination and mistakes a boundary protecting the soul's own relation to the Almighty for a defect he must correct. Love becomes control when the lover decides that no good can exist beyond the lover's administration.

We receive both warnings. Pride need not announce itself as malice. It may speak as urgent benevolence: I alone see what is wrong; I cannot permit the other person's freedom; the world must be perfected by my intervention. A compassionate intention does not make that conclusion harmless. Neither does it erase the genuine care that made the temptation persuasive.

We do not teach that every refusal of authority reenacts the fall. Leaving an abusive household, exposing a religious official, correcting a founder, or resisting a state can be faithful stewardship. Authority proves its legitimacy by service, not by identifying every challenge to itself with Satan. The human who claims to defend God may be defending only the throne that human has built beneath God's name.

The remedy is not self-hatred. A received gift remains a real gift; we need not pretend that skill, intelligence, courage, and beauty are absent. We learn to enjoy their exercise without claiming their ultimate source. We say: this work is mine to answer for, and its possibility is not mine to own. The return of glory does not cancel responsibility. It makes responsibility possible without divinizing the responsible person.
