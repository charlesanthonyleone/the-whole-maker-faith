Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 28. Belonging, welcome, and freely undertaken commitment

We distinguish being welcomed, practicing alongside us, and freely confessing the faith. These are not ranks of human worth. A guest may eat, study, receive aid, ask questions, and return without declaring belief. A practitioner may try the disciplines while remaining uncertain. A member consciously undertakes this faith's theistic orientation and common obligations. Being loved requires no confession.

For membership we ask an adult, in their own words, whether they intend to honor the Whole Maker, pursue the two loves, receive correction, and avoid using the community to possess another person's conscience. We do not demand certainty about every cosmological identification, assent to a political platform, a payment, disclosure of intimate history, or an experience that can be displayed as proof. The distinction is between an honest undertaking and a counterfeit confession.

We propose a welcome witness rather than a sacramental gate. After an unhurried opportunity to read and question, the person states the commitment they can honestly make. Those present state the care and accountability they are willing to offer. A meal, a shared act of service, a song, or silence may accompany it. No washing, mark, oath of secrecy, or imposed new name is required. Anyone may decline a public ceremony and make the undertaking quietly with witnesses of their choosing.

Children belong to the care of the community without being bound by an adult vow. We may bless their arrival and promise protection; we cannot confess on their behalf that they agree with us. As understanding grows, they must be free to question, practice differently, or choose another faith. A parent's membership is not ownership of the child's future conscience.

A member may pause or leave without permission, spiritual threats, harassment, or loss of assistance already promised for a nonreligious need. We may explain our convictions; we may not declare that departure proves the person's soul has become refuse. Relationships may change honestly, but organized shunning must not be used to manufacture dependency.

We ask departing members what they wish done with their records and how they wish future contact handled. We preserve evidence of misconduct where duty requires it, but we do not keep a religious dossier to hold a person inside. A freely formed bond must remain distinguishable from a trap.
