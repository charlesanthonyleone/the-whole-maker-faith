Part II. The human being, the moral world, and the life that continues

Chapter 16. The three courts and the undivided body

We receive the Three Courts as a map for listening: stomach, heart, and mind. Appetite names embodied need and desire. Heart names relational meaning and feeling. Mind names reflection, planning, and the testing of consequences. These are functional images, not three literally separate sovereigns or a clinically established partition of the nervous system.

We refuse the spirituality that seeks holiness by treating the body as an adversary. Hunger, fatigue, attraction, grief, pleasure, and the need for touch are not in themselves sins. They are part of the material through which a life becomes knowable. We also refuse the claim that the body's intensity makes every desired act right. A signal must be heard before it is interpreted; interpretation must consider the other persons affected.

We establish a practice of three questions before a consequential choice. What does my body need or seek? What relationship and meaning are at stake? What does careful reasoning suggest will happen, including to those not present? The answers are spoken or written separately before being brought into conversation. The purpose is not to obtain three unanimous votes, but to prevent one register from impersonating the whole person.

In disagreement, the mind may discover that what it calls discipline is fear of feeling. The heart may discover that what it calls loyalty is fear of abandonment. Appetite may discover a need that can be met without consuming its first object. Each court can learn. None must be exterminated for the others to function.

We do not use masculine and feminine vocabulary to assign rationality exclusively to men or feeling exclusively to women. The source sometimes presents these as functional modes and sometimes attaches them strongly to sexed household roles. That tension remains. Every practitioner must cultivate the capacity to reason, receive, feel, and act. A person's sex is not evidence that a court may be left unheard.

Our hope is not a frictionless inner kingdom. It is a person capable of bringing embodied desire, relational meaning, and accountable reasoning into the presence of God without making any one of them an idol. Prayer begins where the whole person is allowed to arrive.
