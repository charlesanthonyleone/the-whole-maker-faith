Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 36. Memory, ancestors, and the truthful record

We keep memory because the past continues to shape what the living can do. Our obligation is not to make the past flattering. It is to make inheritance intelligible enough that its gifts can continue and its injuries need not be blindly repeated.

A Cry to the Future asks us to preserve monuments as evidence of human making without endorsing everything the makers did. We receive that distinction. A preserved object can be accompanied by a fuller account, a changed public setting, or the testimony of people excluded from its original celebration. Preservation is not the same as compulsory reverence, and contextualization need not be erasure.

We honor ancestors beyond blood: people who maintained water, medicine, roads, libraries, tools, seeds, stories, and languages for those not yet born. We preserve unfinished work as well as polished achievement. A thought ledger, oral history, recipe, recording, sketch, or carefully labeled archive may carry a gift that its first maker could not complete.

Consent governs living memory. No person is required to donate an intimate history to a public archive in order to be faithful. Images, health information, confessions, and children's records need protection. An AI reconstruction of an ancestor must be identified as a reconstruction, not presented as the dead person's actual speech or as contact with their soul. We do not let an engaging simulation acquire the authority of a witness who cannot correct it.

We remember light-bearers across traditions through the specific things they did: an honest refusal, a costly act of care, an argument pursued under pressure, a protection offered where silence would have been safer. The Lost Saints asks us to separate the light from the hand that carried it. We therefore honor a person's genuine witness without canonizing every opinion or concealing misconduct.

We do not require martyrdom as proof of faith. We support endangered witnesses while they live rather than discovering their sanctity after refusing their need. We teach children courage together with the right to seek safety and help. The best memorial may be an institution in which the next truthful person does not have to be abandoned in order to be remembered.
