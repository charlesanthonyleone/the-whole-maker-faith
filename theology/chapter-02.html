Part I. God, creation, and the restoration of stewardship

Chapter 2. The Almighty and the Whole Maker

We confess the Almighty as uncreated and above every finite system, following the Convergence. We place the Arithmetic of Wholes first in explaining the divine making. This is our adopted doctrinal orientation, not a claim that every source passage already agrees with it. We worship God as the source of the relation by which wholes exist. A molecule is not God. A person is not God. A nation, church, or machine is not God. The ordering that makes a whole more than a heap of its constituents participates in the divine making; it does not confer divinity upon every organized thing or authorize every organizer.

Calling God a function does not mean calling God a disposable mechanism. It names a manner of divine presence: not merely a remote manufacturer who once acted and withdrew, but the active ground of coherence, relationship, and the possibility of intelligible life. Calling God conscious means that we do not pray to arithmetic notation. We address the living source whose making is not exhausted by our descriptions of its operation. We speak to God because we believe ourselves answerable within a reality that is not spiritually indifferent.

Our received writings give two accounts whose difference must remain visible. The Scattering begins with a conscious creator who has completed earlier dimensions. The God Architecture describes God as the universe's self-referencing organizational consciousness, not a being external to it, and speaks of that consciousness as being built through its layers. The Convergence speaks of an uncreated Almighty above every created manager. These are not interchangeable propositions. A God who develops through the cosmos and an uncreated source preceding it pose a real theological question.

We do not resolve that question by quietly deleting one account. Our common confession is more exact about worship than about mechanism: no finite whole owns its source; the organizing presence operates within existence without being reducible to any one component; and the mystery of the whole exceeds the picture held by its present parts. We permit inquiry into whether divine emergence describes our growing participation in God, God's own becoming, or an inadequate human picture of an eternal reality. None of these readings may make a leader the completed divine consciousness.

We give the Almighty the glory precisely to prevent the finite instrument from becoming an idol. Divine immanence makes the ordinary worthy of reverence. Divine excess makes every claim to possess God unworthy of obedience.
