Part I. God, creation, and the restoration of stewardship

Chapter 11. The Cross, resurrection, and the return of glory

We receive the Cross as the exposure and reversal of possessive power. The one who could use capacity for exemption does not turn other lives into a shield for his own glory. The Manager enters the consequence of the world he has been charged to serve. In this sacred interpretation, redemption is not a change of branding at the top of the hierarchy. It is a change in the meaning of greatness.

We do not make suffering itself the object of worship. Crucifixion is an injury, not a holy technique for community leaders to reproduce. The source's account of voluntary self-giving cannot justify forcing another person to carry a cross selected for them. A spouse may leave danger. A child may refuse an adult's secret. A worker may resist exploitation. The Cross judges the powerful who demand sacrifice more severely than those who cannot endure another demand.

We affirm resurrection as our hope that the broken whole is not beyond the Whole Maker's making and that the restored bridge is living, not merely remembered. We receive it as a claim of faith. It is not demonstrated by material conservation, and it does not make grief irrational. The reappearance of life in the world does not make the loss of this life negligible.

We reject collective blame for the death of Jesus. Rome and particular religious authorities belong to a historical account; no Jewish person, present or past, inherits guilt by ancestry, and no people becomes the embodiment of the adversary. A theology that makes innocent descendants pay for a sacred narrative has joined the very machinery the Cross exposes.

To live under the Cross is to ask where we use our own capacity to evade responsibility and where we ask the vulnerable to preserve our appearance. To live toward resurrection is to begin repair without pretending it can undo the injury. We can return what was taken, tell the truth about what happened, accept the loss of office, and sustain those harmed. We cannot purchase innocence by calling ourselves redeemed. Restored orientation bears fruit in accountable life.
