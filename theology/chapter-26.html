Founding theology

Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 26. The gathered community

We gather because no person can supply all the witness, correction, consolation, and practical care required for a truthful religious life. Gathering does not make us a superior species. It makes our promises visible to one another and gives them a place to become more than private intention.

We offer a simple, adaptable order for a meeting: acknowledgment of God; room for silence or song; a reading with its source and status named; discussion in which a question may remain unresolved; attention to actual needs and promises; and a closing return of glory. This is a proposed convening form, not sacred law. The source supplies the need for covenant rituals and community witness; it does not supply these exact words or sequence. A household, remote group, or larger assembly may adapt the form without forfeiting fidelity.

A gathering should make clear who is facilitating, how long it is intended to last, how contributions are invited, and where someone may raise a concern privately. We do not demand that everyone speak. We do not turn the gathering into an opportunity to diagnose absent people, expose intimate details, or pressure newcomers into a public declaration. A person's refusal to share is not evidence that something spiritually corrupt is being hidden.

We receive art, food, music, craft, movement, and play as possible forms of common worship. Seriousness is not a prohibition of delight. Nor may atmosphere substitute for understanding. An impressive space, a moving song, or a powerful silence can intensify a false claim as easily as a true one.

We establish no ordained mediator whose presence makes God available. Facilitators and readers have tasks, not superior spiritual substance. Those tasks should pass among capable people, with provision for accessibility, translation, and those who cannot remain for long periods. A meeting that systematically excludes caregivers or those with sensory needs is not whole because the remaining participants feel unified.

The gathering ends with care that has an owner and a limit: who will bring the meal, make the call, repair the item, or accompany the person, and what they have genuinely agreed to do. We will not call an unanswered public request community merely because it was heard in a room full of believers.
