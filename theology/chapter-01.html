Founding theology

Part I. God, creation, and the restoration of stewardship

Chapter 1. The confession and the undertaking

We confess one ultimate source of creation and wholeness, the Almighty, whom these writings call the Whole Maker. We confess that our capacity to understand and form relationships is received rather than self-originating. We undertake to love God with the whole of our being and our neighbor as ourselves. We receive the life, teaching, Cross, and resurrection of Jesus as the decisive pattern of stewardship restored: power refusing to make itself the destination of the life entrusted to it.

This confession is the heart of our worship, not a claim that we have solved every question about divine being. We distinguish what we entrust ourselves to from what we can demonstrate. Faith is neither the replacement of evidence with insistence nor the postponement of life until certainty is complete. It is the responsible commitment of the person to the good that person has encountered, accompanied by a willingness to be corrected about its interpretation.

We undertake four inseparable movements: to receive without contempt, to discern without conceit, to form without consuming, and to return glory without denying responsibility. Receiving alone can become indiscriminate accumulation. Discernment alone can become the endless prosecution of other people. Making alone can become an empire. Gratitude alone can become an excuse never to act. Together they constitute a religious life.

We will not treat spiritual intensity as sufficient evidence of spiritual health. A person may be greatly moved and still mistake fear for revelation. A community may be orderly and still organize itself around a lie. We therefore ask of a teaching both what it says about God and what it trains us to do to our neighbor. No doctrine may purchase its apparent coherence with another person's disappearance.

Our founding promise is not that membership will remove grief, illness, conflict, or error. It is that we will not knowingly abandon one another to them, conceal them to protect our appearance, or convert them into evidence that those who suffer deserve less love. We will make room for truth to arrive before we decide what shape its bearer ought to have.
