Part I. God, creation, and the restoration of stewardship

Chapter 4. Creation, the void, and the first gift

We receive existence as gift before we regard it as inventory. The existence of anything at all is not an entitlement we produced. The world's availability to our attention, our capacity to participate in it, and the prior lives that made our lives possible summon gratitude before mastery.

The Arithmetic of Wholes calls zero the pre-creation void: not an empty room, not a quantum vacuum, but the absence of every variable, field, and relation. It uses this image to distinguish absolute nonbeing from the disappearance of a thing from a particular account. We preserve the distinction. Removing a cup from a table does not remove the cup from reality. Destroying a home does not erase the people, materials, memory, or obligations that belonged to it.

The source also speaks both of the first emergence from zero and of an organizing function continuously drawing new variables from it. Elsewhere the creator already exists and has completed prior worlds. We cannot turn those images into one established chronology. We hold the void as a limit-image of dependence: everything that we can count presupposes a making we did not perform. We do not teach that modern mathematics is invalid because an empty set has cardinality zero, or that cosmology has experimentally observed a state before all existence.

We bless creation in its particularity. Distinction is not the original sin. A world without differences could not have relationship, discovery, gift, or a neighbor to love. The good of unity is not the disappearance of differences into an undifferentiated substance. It is their intelligible communion.

We therefore begin creation teaching with gratitude rather than a demand to win a chronological argument. Children may learn scientific accounts of cosmic and biological development without being told that curiosity betrays God. Adults may investigate the metaphysical question of why there is something rather than nothing without pretending that the presence of a question supplies a scientific answer. The first act of worship is to recognize that what is before us is not ours simply because we can reach it.
