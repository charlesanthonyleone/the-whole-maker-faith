Part II. The human being, the moral world, and the life that continues

Chapter 19. The two loves and the fruit test

We receive the two commandments of Jesus as the governing orientation of every other religious act: love the Almighty with heart, soul, mind, and strength; love the neighbor as oneself. The first prevents the self or its institution from claiming ultimacy. The second prevents devotion to an invisible source from becoming an excuse to neglect visible beings.

Love is not mere approval. It may console, confront, refuse, protect, or ask for a costly change. The Root of Genocide insists that love can be a sword against the lie that consumes the innocent. We receive that sword as truthful witness and protective courage, never as a mandate for assault, intimidation, or holy war. A blanket offered to a cold person is not spiritually inferior because it is gentle. Confrontation offered to protect a public image is not spiritually superior because it is loud.

We establish the fruit test as a discipline with more than one observer. Does this practice increase truthful relationship, real capacity, generosity, and the freedom to seek God? What does it cost the least powerful participant? What happens when someone refuses? What becomes visible over months rather than in the emotion of a single gathering? Would we judge the same outcome favorably if a rival religion produced it?

Fruit is not identical to growth in membership, wealth, reputation, or visible order. Predatory institutions can produce these. Nor is hardship sufficient proof of bad fruit: telling an unwelcome truth may cost a position, and tending an ill person may exhaust resources. We judge the relation between the act, its means, its ends, and those who bear its cost.

We are not entitled to declare a victim's suffering the good fruit of another person's lesson. The sufferer has standing to speak about what occurred, and their experience must not be overridden by the officiant's claim to see a larger spiritual benefit.

The fruit test remains necessary and incomplete. We can misread it, choose flattering measures, or stop observing too early. That is why love requires both a willing heart and procedures that permit unwelcome evidence to reach it. A conscience that cannot be questioned is not a sufficient instrument for governing a community.
