Part II. The human being, the moral world, and the life that continues

Chapter 20. Sin, ignorance, and the counterfeit whole

We call sin a refusal or deformation of right relationship: the use of received life as if it existed only for one's own completion. It includes violence, exploitation, lying, betrayal, deliberate neglect, and the manipulation of sacred authority. It includes the institutional arrangements by which responsibility is dispersed until no official admits to having chosen what the institution repeatedly does.

We distinguish ignorance, incapacity, negligence, and deliberate wrongdoing. They can produce similar injuries while requiring different forms of correction. A person who did not understand may need teaching; a person unable to act differently may need care and changed conditions; a negligent person must acquire the discipline they omitted; a person who knowingly harms must be restrained and held responsible. Understanding the distinction does not erase the injured person's need in any case.

We do not teach inherited personal guilt. Children inherit conditions, practices, and consequences, not culpability merely for being born. They are not spiritual debtors required to redeem their parents. The work of breaking inheritance begins when adults stop making the child carry what adults refuse to examine.

We also distinguish the person from the damaging posture without pretending the posture is unreal. No one is disposable raw material for our moral project. Yet no one is owed continued access to people they endanger because we believe their original nature was good. Hope for restoration is not permission to postpone protection.

Confession begins with the act: what I did, what I knew, whom it affected, what I concealed, and what I am prepared to change. It does not begin with an elaborate account of how the person harmed made the act understandable. Explanations may be necessary later; they must not displace ownership.

The counterfeit whole can also be an identity built around innocence. A practitioner who cannot admit wrongdoing without losing all sense of self will be tempted to attack witnesses. We therefore teach a mercy strong enough to make admission survivable and a consequence serious enough to make admission more than a strategy. The invitation is to cease becoming whole by making another person smaller.
