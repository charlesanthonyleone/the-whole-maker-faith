Part II. The human being, the moral world, and the life that continues

Chapter 14. The arithmetic of living wholes

We receive the Arithmetic of Wholes as a spiritual discipline of accounting. Before declaring that something has been removed, we ask where its constituents, effects, and obligations have gone. Before declaring that something has been added, we ask whether a functioning relationship has actually been formed. A collection is not necessarily communion; dispersal is not absolute erasure.

Formation joins distinct capacities so that a new whole can do what its participants could not do alone. Dissolution releases a configuration that no longer holds. Re-formation brings what remains into new relationships. These operations describe a pattern we can recognize in a meal, a work of art, a household, or the recovery of a community. They do not imply that every mixture is fruitful or every dissolution is good.

The source itself corrects its opening claim that subtraction is an illusion. It later allows real subtraction of a part from a whole and real multiplication, while denying that the removed material becomes absolute nothing. We retain this correction as part of the teaching. It exemplifies truthfulness: a clearer account may revise a powerful opening sentence. We will not make loyalty require repeating the sentence after learning the distinction.

We also distinguish physical persistence from the persistence of a particular organization. A burned library leaves matter and energy; it may lose texts forever. A killed person leaves material and effects; these are not a substitute for that person's company. We cannot infer from conservation that no irreparable loss occurs. The permanence of consequence makes destruction more serious, not less.

Our rule is therefore to preserve the living whole where it remains good, repair where repair is possible, release where a bond is harmful or exhausted, and receive what remains without pretending that the loss never happened. Reconciliation is not always reunion. A person can be forgiven without being restored to access, office, or intimacy.

God's math, as a religious phrase, asks us to attend to all affected scales. A community cannot report its increase while hiding the diminishment of its members. A member cannot report personal freedom while exporting its cost to children, neighbors, or a poisoned river. We hold a page, not the ledger. Honest stewardship keeps that page open to what it has omitted.
