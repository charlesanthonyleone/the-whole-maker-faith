Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 33. Healing, distress, and the limits of spiritual explanation

We receive the Alarm and the Signal's demand to hear distress in relation to a life. Sleep, safety, meaningful activity, social connection, bodily needs, and the burden of survival can shape suffering. A religious community that offers only words while refusing practical care has ignored the very relational world it calls sacred.

We do not receive the source's broad clinical claims as established medical truth. IRBAD, Neurochronological Imbalance Syndrome, function-alignment predictions, Alpine Divorce Syndrome, and Polarity Suppression Syndrome are hypotheses or proposed frameworks, not validated diagnoses supplied by this theology. Differences in brain development, the significance of attachment, and the reality of environmental harm do not establish every further mechanism or treatment conclusion the writings propose.

Depression, anxiety, psychosis, addiction, and other conditions can have multiple interacting causes. Distress is not always an accurate report of an external threat or a single misaligned vocation. Medication and evidence-based therapy can be important and lifesaving. We do not direct anyone to stop treatment, interpret a prescription as spiritual suppression, or make professional care conditional on religious participation. A minister's language of spirit is not a license to practice medicine.

We can offer a healing environment within our competence: consistent presence, practical help, reduced isolation, room to rest, and respectful accompaniment to appropriate care. We can ask what is happening around a symptom without claiming to have diagnosed its cause. We can pray without promising cure. We can remain when improvement is slow, partial, or absent.

The Belgian Malinois Principle asks what an intense passion or vice may be seeking and how a destructive channel might be transformed into responsible relation. We receive that question without teaching that dangerous drug use is a hidden sacrament or that addiction can be mastered by studying it religiously. Recovery and harm reduction may require medical and specialist support. Abstaining from a substance can be the most faithful way to honor the life beneath its attraction.

A vision, voice, dream, or unusual experience may be important to the person reporting it; it is not automatically a message from God. We receive the person without prematurely certifying an explanation, attend to safety and functioning, and support care where needed. Faith must not turn a vulnerable person's crisis into a stage for the community's expectations.
