Part II. The human being, the moral world, and the life that continues

Chapter 21. The five patterns of moral law

We receive Beneath the Statute's five patterns as disciplines of judgment: causation, reciprocity, witness, property, and hierarchy. They do not constitute an automatic oracle that identifies the guilty or a substitute for lawful institutions. They ask what a proposed judgment has failed to count.

Causation requires us to follow the actual path from act to injury. Intention matters, but does not make effects vanish. An undesirable outcome does not alone prove a prior sin. Illness, poverty, assault, and bereavement are not reliable evidence that their sufferers violated a spiritual law.

Reciprocity requires us to notice what is received, what is returned, and where a relation survives by extracting from those unable to refuse. It does not mean that every gift must be repaid by the person who receives it. Infants, the dying, and people in crisis need forms of love that are not transactions. The circulation can pass through many lives and generations. We must not promise that generosity guarantees prosperity or that every unpaid moral account will visibly settle within our lifetime.

Witness requires us to examine standpoint, opportunity, memory, interest, corroboration, and the conditions under which someone speaks. A witness reveals something of themselves, but an imperfect person may still report a real injury. A respected official may still lie. We do not replace investigation with a reputation contest.

Property requires us to recognize that holding creates duties. What one claims must be tended, and the more significant the claim, the greater the obligation. A person is never a form of religious property. Neither donations nor years of service confer ownership of another member's conscience.

Hierarchy requires the summit to honor the foundation. Capacity may justify a coordinating responsibility; it does not justify treating those coordinated as lesser souls. The higher the office, the more explicit its duties of disclosure, correction, and removal. A founder is not exempt from the laws by being the person who first wrote them.

Together these patterns make religious consequence more exacting and less theatrical. We ask who acted, what happened, what repair is possible, what protection is needed, and who is competent to decide. We do not call a verdict divine merely because its author felt certain.
