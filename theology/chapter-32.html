Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 32. Children, reproductive responsibility, and the inheritance we must interrupt

We place children's care before adult competition over religious recognition. A child is not an exhibit proving the excellence of a doctrine, a mediator between parents, a reward owed to a household, or a spiritual debtor born to continue a founder's project. The community's promises must be judged by the actual life of the child who receives them.

Breaking the Inheritance teaches us to ask what behavior is communicating and what the adult must become in order to respond. Discipline teaches skills, limits, repair, and increasingly responsible freedom. Punishment that only produces fear teaches concealment and hands an injury to the next generation. We stop dangerous behavior promptly, protect those harmed, and then investigate need, environment, learning, and accountability. Compassion does not require allowing harm to continue.

We do not infer from a child's difficult behavior that a particular parent must have caused it, or from a parent's exhaustion that the parent is spiritually defective. Broad source claims about inherited injury are calls to examine conditions, not forensic proof in an individual case. A community that believes care must be distributed must actually offer respite, food, safe companionship, and practical assistance instead of merely diagnosing the caregiver.

We approach conceiving and bearing children as responsibilities toward emerging life, the embodied person who bears it, existing children, and the sustaining household. The command to fill creation with loving life is not a command to maximize births irrespective of care. We reject reproductive coercion, sexual entitlement, and the use of fertility or infertility as evidence of divine favor. Loss in pregnancy deserves care, not an inquiry into whether someone prayed correctly.

Our detailed reproductive theology is unfinished. IntelOasis does not provide a developed ruling on abortion, contraception, assisted reproduction, gestational thresholds, or competing obligations in medical emergencies. The dignity of children and the persistence of material existence do not by themselves settle those questions. We will not smuggle a political position into the religion under the pretense that a metaphor has already decided it. Clinically urgent decisions require qualified care, not delay while an elder improvises divine law.

We commit now to what is clear: truthful consent, protection from abuse, care before and after birth, support for adoptive and other caregivers, and independent attention to children's interests when adults disagree. Unfinished doctrine is not permission for unfinished care.
