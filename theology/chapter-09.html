Part I. God, creation, and the restoration of stewardship

Chapter 9. Every religion that followed

We receive the religious inheritance of humanity as more than a collection of errors awaiting our correction. Each tradition carries encounters, disciplines, and perceptions we did not produce. The question is not which people may be reduced to our categories, but what we can receive truthfully from them and what our own convictions require us to question.

The Scattering reads Norse stories of Tyr, Odin, and Loki; Egyptian accounts of Atum, Osiris, Set, and Horus; and Greek narratives of the Olympians and excluded figures as traces of disrupted stewardship. Its novel proposes additional Mesopotamian, Hindu, and Mesoamerican perspectives. We receive those comparisons as the originating writer's proposed reading. We do not present etymology, council size, or a similarity between two deaths as proof that the deities are historically or metaphysically identical. Ancient traditions are not waiting rooms whose only value is their resemblance to us.

We receive from Jewish traditions their sustained work of covenant, memory, argument, and justice; from Christian traditions the witness of incarnation, forgiveness, and costly love; from Islamic traditions rigorous divine unity, prayer, and surrender; from Hindu traditions profound cosmological and contemplative exploration; from Buddhist traditions exacting attention to suffering, attachment, and the mind; from Jain traditions radical moral attention to living beings; from Sikh traditions devotion joined to service and an open table; from Taoist and Confucian traditions attention to way, relation, and cultivated conduct. These are invitations to study, not definitions of entire peoples or claims that a tradition has only one gift.

We receive living Indigenous traditions in their own names and places, not as an interchangeable storehouse of ecological symbols. We receive African and African-diasporic religions, Zoroastrian traditions, Bahá'í communities, new religious movements, and traditions we have not yet learned to name. We neither certify every doctrine nor exclude a people because they are absent from our present map. Their own accounts have priority over our convenient summaries.

No list completes the gathering. Every reception must ask permission where a practice is restricted, name what has been borrowed, and admit when a claimed equivalence is not accepted by those whose faith we are describing. Universal invitation is not universal ownership.
