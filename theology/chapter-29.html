Founding theology

Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 29. Rites of change without a captive life

We mark transitions because a community should be able to witness change rather than force its members to carry it alone. We do not claim that our rites are required for God to notice a birth, promise, rupture, illness, or death. They exist to change our attention and clarify our responsibility.

At a birth or adoption, we welcome the child without predicting a spiritual role. We ask who will support the caregivers, what care is missing, and what promise each witness is actually able to keep. The Welcome Dish and First Root may accompany this reception. We celebrate the arrival without making fertility a measure of holiness or adoptive kinship a secondary form of family.

At a coming of responsibility, we witness a capacity that has genuinely developed: care for a living thing, understanding of a tool, reliable service, or the ability to make and review a promise. We do not manufacture a dramatic test. An age or birthday may provide occasion, but maturity is not established by enduring fear before an audience.

At a household covenant or its transition, the participants state what has changed, what they promise, and what is not promised. The witnesses ask whether each person can refuse and whether dependents have been considered. No ceremony converts an unlawful arrangement into a legal marriage or cancels existing civil obligations. The religion offers witness; it does not pretend to hold the state's authority.

At a rupture, we may mourn the whole that no longer holds. We do not require both parties to attend together, disclose intimate causes, or enact reconciliation. We acknowledge what was real, what must cease, and what obligations continue. A broken covenant must not be hidden merely to preserve an attractive image, but truth can be held in protected forms rather than public spectacle.

At illness, we offer presence, practical aid, and prayer without a promise of cure. At death, we commend the person to God, support the bereaved, and arrange continuing care. A rite that ends with beautiful words and leaves urgent needs unassigned has not completed its work.

These are proposed forms for common practice. They can be revised, declined, or carried in culturally different ways. We do not place the form above the persons for whom it was made.
