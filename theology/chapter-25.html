Part III. Worship, household, and the practices of a living faith

Chapter 25. An ordinary rule of life

We establish a rule that may be practiced without specialist equipment, purchased access, unusual experiences, or withdrawal from ordinary duties. Its elements are attention, study, service, maintenance, and return. Its rhythm is chosen by the practitioner in relation to health, work, care, and the actual shape of a life. We institute no compulsory prayer hours and no reading calendar.

Attention means allowing some honest encounter with the present condition before distraction takes command: a few quiet breaths, a walk, a written question, or an acknowledgment of God. Study means returning to a source deeply enough to be corrected, rather than consuming religious phrases for reassurance. Service means one actual movement toward another's need, not merely a public declaration of concern. Maintenance means tending something already entrusted to us. Return means examining what happened and releasing the claim that its fruit exists solely for our reputation.

These elements need not occupy five separate sessions. Preparing food while listening carefully to a child may join attention, service, and maintenance. Studying a damaged tool and repairing it for a neighbor may join study and offering. The rule is intended to reveal worship within life, not add an impossible second life to those already carrying too much.

We encourage a modest record of practice where it helps. A person may note a question, an act, a promise, a mistake, or something received. The record belongs to the practitioner. No elder may demand access as proof of obedience. A missed day does not accumulate a spiritual fine. Habit is useful because it supports faithfulness; it becomes corrupt when its streak matters more than the person it was meant to support.

Rest is included in maintenance. Sleep, unstructured play, bodily care, and time without performance are not concessions to weakness. A community that continually asks members to spend themselves until they can no longer love has mistaken consumption for offering.

When the rule becomes burdensome, we simplify it: tell one truth, tend one thing, receive one kindness without attempting to earn it. The point is not to become a flawless practitioner. It is to remain available to God and neighbor in a life that can continue.
