Part IV. The community's limits, its witnesses, and its service to the world

Chapter 41. Accountability, protection, and repair

We establish accountability because a community built only on good intentions will eventually ask injured people to pay for its innocence. We do not exempt ourselves from patterns we readily recognize in other institutions. A religious body is especially dangerous when it can interpret every complaint as a spiritual defect in the complainant.

A person reporting harm must be able to speak to someone outside the accused person's authority. Immediate safety comes before mediation. We attend to children, dependency, retaliation, financial control, and the possibility that a request to keep the peace is protecting the person with more power. We comply with applicable reporting duties and do not treat confession, membership, or promised internal repair as immunity from lawful investigation.

We distinguish pastoral accompaniment from fact-finding. A person can receive care while a claim is being investigated; care need not wait for a final verdict. An accusation also requires fair examination rather than ritual certainty. We seek relevant evidence, hear responses through safe channels, record conflicts of interest, and provide review by people not dependent on the outcome. We do not require an alleged victim to face an accused person directly as proof of sincerity.

Community consequences are bounded: restriction from a task or space, removal from office, a plan for restitution, or an end to participation where safety requires it. They are not corporal punishment, detention, seizure of property, forced labor, or a declaration about the person's eternal destiny. We do not establish a criminal jurisdiction. State punishment, including the death penalty where a state uses it, is never God's judgment on a soul by our certification.

Repair requires naming the act, ceasing it, acknowledging affected people, returning what can be returned, and accepting safeguards. An apology does not entitle the speaker to forgiveness, resumed contact, or public rehabilitation. Forgiveness belongs to those harmed in the form and time they can honestly offer; it cannot be ordered to make a process look complete.

We reject public confession as entertainment, humiliating exposure, or an unreviewable digital record of a person's worst moment. Necessary transparency must be distinguished from spectacle. Records must protect children and victims and remain subject to correction and appropriate retention limits.

The institution itself can owe repair. We must be prepared to name a false teaching, remove a practice, compensate where possible, and preserve the history of how we failed. An apology that protects the structure from change is another form of the counterfeit whole.
