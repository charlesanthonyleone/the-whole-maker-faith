The Faith of the Whole Maker

God is the Whole Maker, not the largest possessor. We seek communion rather than possession.

Preamble

We gather to worship the God by whom things become whole, and to become people through whom that making can continue without becoming a pretext for possession. We bring what we have received: our ancestors, our languages, our sciences, our prayers, our injured histories, our capacities for understanding, and the questions we have been afraid to ask. We do not bring them to be ground into a powder. We bring them to be built into living relationship.

We begin with a distinction. A steward receives a power and returns its fruit to a world larger than the steward. A possessor takes the received power for its own source, consumes what it was entrusted to tend, and calls the resulting enlargement completeness. The first is communion; the second is the counterfeit of communion. God is not the largest possessor. God is the Whole Maker, the source and living organizing presence through whom genuine communion is possible.

When we are asked what is to be formed, we answer: everything. Every culture, nation, and ideology arrives bearing a brick: something discovered, endured, remembered, or made intelligible. Not every claim within a brick is sound, and no human institution is beyond correction. But a person's inheritance must not be pulverized as the price of being received. Nor will a pile of intact bricks become a house merely because we congratulate ourselves on having assembled them. The mortar is God: the living bond, the intelligible order, the love that makes distinct things capable of sustaining one another.

We receive IntelOasis as the originating body of writings from which this theology grows. We do not claim that its author dictated every rule established here, that its speculative narratives have become demonstrated history, or that these pages are an infallible revelation. Where we establish rites, offices, and procedures, we are giving this inheritance a proposed religious form. The form must answer to what it serves. No signature, founder, assembly, or text takes the place of God.

We are founding a faith, not announcing a government. Our prayer is not a citizenship examination. Our discipline is not a criminal code. Our belonging is not membership in a political party. We intend a religion serious enough to worship, intelligible enough to question, embodied enough to practice, and humble enough to receive what it has not yet learned.
