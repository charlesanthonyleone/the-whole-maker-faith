Receiving other faiths

Chapter 38 and Chapter 9 follow in full, one after another. They are not combined here into a single account.

Chapter 38. Receiving other faiths without possessing them

We enter relationship with other faiths as a confessing community, not as a neutral tribunal above them. We have convictions they may reject. We owe them enough honesty to state those convictions and enough humility to hear what their rejection reveals about our reading.

We receive traditions as bricks, not dust and not a pile. Grinding to dust is forced conversion, appropriation, or the demand that people abandon the living structure of their inheritance so we can use its attractive pieces. Stacking without mortar is the opposite failure: declaring incompatible claims identical, refusing discernment, and calling an assortment a spiritual home. We seek a bond that leaves the received traditions recognizable and opens a relationship none could make alone.

The mortar is God. In The Beginning describes the Morningstar union as the mortar, while Charles's later formation instruction names God and, for the atheist's honest recognition, mathematics. We read the union as a proposed account of God's binding through complementary love, not as a second ultimate source. We record that this is an interpretive decision and that the texts name the bond differently. We do not solve their difference by omitting one.

Our reception begins with depth in the other tradition's own language and history. We ask living adherents what we have misunderstood. We distinguish public teachings from restricted practices. We do not baptize every deity, sage, or philosophical principle into our hierarchy against the account of its own worshippers. Agreement in conduct may be possible where metaphysical disagreement remains.

The source's Melchizedek and symbolic Israel/Zion contrast warns against making open truth tribal property. We apply it first to ourselves. It is not an indictment of Jews or any contemporary people. We do not use sacred narrative as historical evidence of inherited group corruption. A Christian church, a secular institution, or this community can hoard truth; a Jewish community can exemplify generous reception. Conduct, not bloodline, is at issue.

We may pray separately and serve together. We may share a table without pretending every participant has confessed our God. We may seek conversion through truthful invitation, but never through threats, dependency, concealed persuasion, or the price of aid. A faith offered to everyone must remain capable of hearing no.

Chapter 9. Every religion that followed

We receive the religious inheritance of humanity as more than a collection of errors awaiting our correction. Each tradition carries encounters, disciplines, and perceptions we did not produce. The question is not which people may be reduced to our categories, but what we can receive truthfully from them and what our own convictions require us to question.

The Scattering reads Norse stories of Tyr, Odin, and Loki; Egyptian accounts of Atum, Osiris, Set, and Horus; and Greek narratives of the Olympians and excluded figures as traces of disrupted stewardship. Its novel proposes additional Mesopotamian, Hindu, and Mesoamerican perspectives. We receive those comparisons as the originating writer's proposed reading. We do not present etymology, council size, or a similarity between two deaths as proof that the deities are historically or metaphysically identical. Ancient traditions are not waiting rooms whose only value is their resemblance to us.

We receive from Jewish traditions their sustained work of covenant, memory, argument, and justice; from Christian traditions the witness of incarnation, forgiveness, and costly love; from Islamic traditions rigorous divine unity, prayer, and surrender; from Hindu traditions profound cosmological and contemplative exploration; from Buddhist traditions exacting attention to suffering, attachment, and the mind; from Jain traditions radical moral attention to living beings; from Sikh traditions devotion joined to service and an open table; from Taoist and Confucian traditions attention to way, relation, and cultivated conduct. These are invitations to study, not definitions of entire peoples or claims that a tradition has only one gift.

We receive living Indigenous traditions in their own names and places, not as an interchangeable storehouse of ecological symbols. We receive African and African-diasporic religions, Zoroastrian traditions, Bahá'í communities, new religious movements, and traditions we have not yet learned to name. We neither certify every doctrine nor exclude a people because they are absent from our present map. Their own accounts have priority over our convenient summaries.

No list completes the gathering. Every reception must ask permission where a practice is restricted, name what has been borrowed, and admit when a claimed equivalence is not accepted by those whose faith we are describing. Universal invitation is not universal ownership.
